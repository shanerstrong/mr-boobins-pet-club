# Creative direction and taste memory

Updated: 2026-08-16

## Purpose

Turn Mark's explicit likes, dislikes, approvals, and rejections into durable production evidence. This record governs subjective visual and creative work for the game and should be consulted before making characters, rooms, skins, icons, illustrations, animation, or promotional media.

Objective validation and creative approval are separate gates. An artifact can load correctly, meet its file budget, and use the right rig while still failing the visual direction.

## Authority and status

Only Mark can assign **accepted** or **locked** status to subjective creative work.

| Status | Meaning | Required action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rejected | Mark rejected the artifact, method, or direction. | Stop using that direction. Do not polish or quietly retry it. Propose a materially different approach, reference set, or tool before more production. |
| Exploratory | A deliberately cheap draft made to answer a specific question. | Show it honestly, including defects. Do not present it as endorsed or production-ready. |
| Provisional | It satisfies named objective or technical checks, but Mark has not approved the look. | It may support a bounded test only when clearly labeled; it cannot become the creative source of truth. |
| Accepted | Mark explicitly approved the direction for the stated use. | Preserve the approved qualities and use it as the basis for the next bounded step. |
| Locked | Mark explicitly approved it as the source of truth. | Changes require a new comparison and approval. |

Codex may say that an artifact **passes named objective checks** and may describe measurable differences. Codex must not call its own subjective output good, great, polished, professional, accurate-looking, approved, or successful unless that judgment is attributed to Mark's explicit approval.

## Feedback protocol

1. Show the candidate beside its approved reference at fixed, useful views whenever possible.
2. Identify visible defects and uncertainty before asking for approval; do not sell the output.
3. Ask for judgment at the cheapest useful checkpoint: first silhouette and likeness, then style and proportion, then production detail, rigging, animation, and variants.
4. Treat one clear rejection as a stop signal for that method or direction. More effort does not turn the same rejected approach into approval.
5. Record the exact artifact, date, status, what Mark said he liked or hated, what to preserve or avoid, and the next allowed approach.
6. Do not invent a reason for Mark's reaction. Ask only when the missing reason would materially change the next approach.
7. A technical pass never overrides a creative rejection. A creative approval never replaces technical, accessibility, safety, or licensing checks.
